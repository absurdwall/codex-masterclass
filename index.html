The learning arc

Understand a little.
Make something. Go further.

Start with enough context to feel oriented, then learn through a small creation. Use that experience to explore bigger possibilities.

01 / ORIENT00–10 MIN · GUIDED TOUR

Find your bearings

Build a simple mental map before opening every menu.

Why it belongs

A little orientation makes the hands-on part easier: where work lives, what you can adjust, and how to ask for a useful result.

What we’ll explore

  • Models: how to approach choosing one
  • Projects: keeping related work together
  • Settings and features worth knowing first
LEAVE THIS SECTION WITH

“I know where to start, and where to look when I get stuck.”

Models guide ↗Projects guide ↗

Keep it focused: a useful starting map, with deeper settings left for reference. Model availability can vary by account.

02 / CREATE10–30 MIN · HANDS-ON

Make a personal pet

A playful first project with a result you can recognize as your own.

Why it belongs

A small creative task makes the core loop tangible: describe an idea, inspect what happens, and ask for a specific improvement.

The proposed activity

  1. Choose a character and a personality
  2. Describe its look and a few defining details
  3. Review the result and refine one thing
A STARTING IDEA

“Create a tiny moss-green creature with round ears, curious eyes, and a cheerful bounce.”

Then make one detail unmistakably yours.
Outcome: a first pet, plus one iterationPets guide ↗
03 / CONNECT30–40 MIN · SHORT WALKTHROUGH

Meet dots & ChatGPT Space

Place the new pieces in the bigger picture.

Why it belongs

After making something, zoom out. Introduce dots and Space through one concrete scenario so the audience can see where each might fit.

The proposed walkthrough

  • Introduce dots and their role
  • Show a Space and how it organizes work
  • Connect the examples to a familiar personal project
THE GUIDING QUESTION

“Where would this help me in my own day?”

Dots guide ↗Space guide ↗

05 / OBSERVE48–56 MIN · LIVE DEMO

Watch it take action

Use a simple card game to make computer use visible.

Why it belongs

A familiar game gives the audience a clear way to follow an action, observe the result, and spot when a correction is needed.

The proposed demonstration

  • Set a bounded task in a free, non-wagering game
  • Watch a short sequence of computer actions
  • Discuss what to check and when to intervene
  • Introduce scheduled tasks with a separate simple example
